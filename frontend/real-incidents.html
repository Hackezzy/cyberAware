Real Incidents

Every simulation on this site uses a fictional brand, so it's always safe to practice on. These five are the opposite — real, well-documented attacks against real organizations, included here for the thing fiction can't provide: proof of what these patterns actually cost when they work. Each one links back to the matching simulation, and every fact below is cited — nothing on this page is written from memory.

The Podesta Campaign Email Hack (2016)

Email & Message Security

What happened

On March 19, 2016, Russian military-intelligence hackers ("Fancy Bear") sent John Podesta, chairman of Hillary Clinton's presidential campaign, an email with the subject line "Someone has your password," disguised as a Google security warning.

How it actually unfolded

The email told Podesta someone had tried to sign into his Google account and urged him to change his password immediately via a link. A staffer forwarded it to the campaign's IT help desk to check whether it was legitimate. The help desk replied that it was a "legitimate email" and that Podesta should change his password — despite the link actually pointing to a shortened URL controlled by the attackers, not Google. Podesta clicked it and entered his real password on the fake page.

Warning signs, in hindsight

A generic "someone has your password" urgency hook; a link that didn't actually go to a real Google domain; and the fact that Google itself never asks you to click an email link to "verify" a password reset — the same red flags taught in the Phishing simulation on this site.

Real-world cost: over 50,000 emails were exfiltrated from Podesta's account within two days and later published by WikiLeaks, becoming a major factor in the 2016 U.S. presidential election.

The Fake "Google Docs" OAuth Worm (2017)

Email & Message Security

What happened

A phishing email arrived from someone the recipient actually knew, inviting them to open a shared Google Doc — a completely normal, everyday-looking email.

How it actually unfolded

Clicking "Open in Docs" sent victims to the real accounts.google.com, but instead of opening a document, it asked them to grant account permissions to an app named "Google Docs" — which was not Google's own app, just a malicious one that had given itself the same name. Anyone who approved it handed over an OAuth token letting the attacker read, send, and manage their email — no password was ever stolen or needed. The worm then used that access to email the same invitation to every one of the victim's Gmail contacts automatically, spreading itself without the attacker doing anything further.

Warning signs, in hindsight

A permission-request screen for an app claiming to be "Google Docs" (the real Google Docs never asks for account permissions to open a shared file) — a reminder that "the page looks real" isn't the same as "the request makes sense."

Real-world cost: roughly 0.1% of all Gmail users were affected before Google disabled the offending accounts and pushed updates through Safe Browsing — at Gmail's scale, a fraction of a percent was still a very large number of real compromised accounts, spreading within roughly an hour of the campaign starting.

WannaCry Ransomware (2017)

Malware & Ransomware

What happened

Starting May 12, 2017, a ransomware worm called WannaCry spread across the internet, encrypting files on infected Windows computers and demanding a ransom to unlock them.

How it actually unfolded

WannaCry used a stolen exploit called EternalBlue to attack a flaw in how Windows shares files over a network — a flaw Microsoft had already released a patch for two months earlier. Computers that hadn't installed that update could be infected without anyone clicking anything; the malware simply found them over the network and spread on its own. Within three days it had reached an estimated 200,000 computers across more than 150 countries, including large parts of the UK's National Health Service, forcing hospitals to divert patients and cancel appointments.

Warning signs, in hindsight

Not a phishing click at all this time — the actual lesson is that an available security update, left uninstalled, is itself the vulnerability. The same "how ransomware spreads and locks a system" idea is what the Ransomware simulation on this site walks through.

Real-world cost: estimated global losses between $4–8 billion. For the NHS alone, the UK Department of Health put the cost at £92 million, with nearly 19,000 cancelled appointments and operations extrapolated across the health service.

The LinkedIn Password Breach (2012)

Identity & Access

What happened

Attackers broke into LinkedIn's systems and stole hashed passwords for what LinkedIn first estimated at 6.5 million accounts. In 2016, the true scale became clear when the full stolen dataset — over 117 million account email/password combinations — was found for sale online.

How it actually unfolded

The passwords had been hashed, but using an outdated method that was relatively easy for attackers to crack back into plain text. The real damage happened years later and elsewhere: anyone who had reused their LinkedIn password on other sites was now exposed everywhere they'd reused it, not just on LinkedIn. In one widely reported case, attackers used credentials from this exact breach to break into Mark Zuckerberg's Twitter and Pinterest accounts in 2016 — four years after the original LinkedIn breach — simply because the same password had been reused across all three.

Warning signs, in hindsight

There was nothing to spot in the moment — this is exactly why credential reuse is dangerous: the breach that exposes your password and the account it actually gets used against can be years and completely unrelated services apart.

Real-world cost: 117+ million accounts exposed, a second forced password-reset wave in 2016, and years of downstream account takeovers on unrelated platforms traced back to this one breach.

Maps to: Credential Reuse Check (Password Strength tool) Source: Krebs on Security, "As Scope of 2012 Breach Expands, LinkedIn to Again Reset Passwords"

The Ubiquiti Networks Invoice Fraud (2015)

Email & Message Security

What happened

Ubiquiti Networks, a real networking hardware company, discovered on June 5, 2015 that its finance department had been tricked into wiring $46.7 million to overseas bank accounts controlled by criminals.

How it actually unfolded

Attackers impersonated senior company executives by email — a classic Business Email Compromise ("CEO fraud") — and used that fake authority to instruct employees in a Hong Kong subsidiary's finance team to transfer company funds to accounts the attackers controlled. No malware, no hacked server — just a convincing email and someone with wire-transfer authority who believed it.

Warning signs, in hindsight

An urgent, executive-authority wire-transfer request outside normal process — exactly the pattern the Business Email Compromise simulation on this site walks through, including why verifying such a request through a second channel (a phone call, not a reply to the same email thread) would have stopped it.

Real-world cost: $46.7 million transferred; only $8.1 million was ever recovered. The company's Chief Accounting Officer resigned after an internal audit found "material weaknesses" in financial controls.
