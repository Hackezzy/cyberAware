Business Email Compromise Simulation

Step through a CEO/invoice fraud scam aimed at a small business — one of the costliest scams for small businesses specifically. Nothing here is real; "Meridian Bookkeeping" is the same made-up small business from the Ransomware walkthrough.

Step 1 of 6

1. The Research

Before sending anything, the attacker quietly studies the company using public information.

Publicly available (LinkedIn, company website)

Owner: Alan Cho, Founder & CEO, Meridian Bookkeeping

Accounts contact: listed on the "Contact Us" page

Recent post: "Alan is traveling to our Denver client site this week!"

What's happening

  • • None of this required hacking anything — it's all publicly posted information, freely available to anyone.
  • • Knowing the CEO is traveling is especially useful: it explains why he'd be emailing instead of walking over to ask in person.

2. The Impersonation

An email arrives in the bookkeeper's inbox, appearing to be from Alan.

From: "Alan Cho" <alan.cho@meridian-bookkeeping.com>

(Meridian's real domain is meridianbooks-corp.com)

What's happening

The domain is one word different from the real one — close enough that it reads fine at a glance in a busy inbox, especially on a phone where the full address often isn't even shown.

3. The Ask

The email requests an urgent wire transfer to a "new vendor."

"Hi — I'm finalizing a deal with a new supplier and need to send them a deposit today before I lose the contract. Can you wire $18,400 to the account below? I'm in back-to-back meetings so email is easiest. Thanks, Alan"

What's happening

  • • A new, unverified vendor and an unusual, one-time wire amount are both classic warning signs on their own — together, they're a strong pattern.
  • • "I'm in back-to-back meetings" pre-emptively explains away the one thing that would normally catch this: picking up the phone.

4. The Pressure

A follow-up email adds secrecy and urgency to discourage double-checking.

"Please keep this between us for now — don't want the team asking questions before the deal is signed. Need this sent in the next hour if possible."

What's happening

Real business decisions this size are rarely both secret and urgent at the same time — that specific combination exists almost entirely to prevent anyone from having time to verify it.

5. The Transfer

Wanting to be responsive to the CEO, the bookkeeper sends the wire.

[wire transfer sent] $18,400.00

[status] completed — funds unavailable for recall

What's happening

Unlike a credit card charge, a wire transfer typically can't be reversed once it clears — there's no dispute process to fall back on afterward.

6. The Aftermath

Business email compromise is one of the costliest scams targeting small businesses — here's how to actually prevent it.

Because a single successful transfer can be tens of thousands of dollars and is rarely recoverable, this is one of the highest-cost scam categories per incident for small businesses — even though it takes only one convincing email.

Prevention checklist

  • • Verify any unusual payment request through a separate, already-known channel — call the person on a number you already have, don't reply to the email or use a number it provides.
  • • Treat "keep this secret" plus "urgent" together as a red flag on its own, regardless of who it appears to be from.
  • • Require a second person's approval for wire transfers above a set amount, so no single email can authorize one alone.
  • • Double-check the sender's actual domain, not just the display name, especially for anything involving money.
