Fake Wi-Fi Simulation

Step through how a rogue Wi-Fi hotspot can intercept everything you send, from the fake network appearing in a list to your login details being captured. This walkthrough is set in a made-up airport lounge.

Step 1 of 6

1. The Setup

An attacker sits in an airport lounge with a laptop, broadcasting a Wi-Fi network with a name designed to sound official.

Attacker's device

Broadcasting SSID: "Airport_Free_WiFi"

No password required — open network

What's happening

  • • The network name copies the wording real venues use, and requires no password, which makes it look convenient rather than suspicious.
  • • Setting up a rogue access point takes nothing more than a laptop — no special access to the building's real network is needed.

2. The Connection

A traveler's phone shows a list of nearby networks. The fake one looks identical to a real one would.

Airport_Free_WiFi

🔓 Open

Airport_Free_WiFi

🔓 Open

CoffeeCart_Guest

🔒 Secured

What's happening

  • • Two networks with the exact same name is itself a red flag — but most phones just show the name, so it's easy to connect to the wrong one without noticing.
  • • A network name alone proves nothing about who actually controls it.

3. The Funnel

Once connected, every request from the phone passes through the attacker's device first.

Your phone → Attacker's laptop → The internet

What's happening

This is called a "man-in-the-middle" position — the attacker doesn't need to break into your phone at all. Every website still loads normally, so there's nothing that looks obviously wrong.

4. The Interception

The traveler opens a work webmail login page over an unencrypted connection.

http://mail.meridianbooks-corp.com/login

[attacker log] captured: user=bookkeeper@meridianbooks-corp.com

[attacker log] captured: pass=•••••••••••

What's happening

  • • The address starts with http://, not https:// — without that encryption, anything typed on the page can be read by whoever is in the middle.
  • • Modern sites mostly use HTTPS by default, but older internal tools, some older webmail portals, or spoofed pages sometimes don't force it.

5. The Catch

The attacker now has a working email login — and the traveler has no idea anything happened.

The webmail page loaded normally and logged them in without any error. From the traveler's side, everything worked exactly as expected.

What's happening

This is what makes rogue Wi-Fi dangerous: there's no popup, no crash, no visible sign anything went wrong. The only real clue was back in step 4 — the missing padlock in the address bar.

6. The Aftermath

Public Wi-Fi is genuinely risky — here's how to actually stay safe on it.

Independent research has found that a significant share of people who use public Wi-Fi have had their security compromised while doing so — this isn't a rare or theoretical scenario.

Prevention checklist

  • • Ask staff for the exact network name before connecting — don't just pick the one that looks right.
  • • Use a VPN on public networks, so your traffic is encrypted even if the network itself isn't trustworthy.
  • • Check for https:// and a padlock icon before entering anything sensitive — and don't proceed past a browser warning about an invalid certificate.
  • • Turn off Wi-Fi auto-connect for open networks, so your device doesn't silently rejoin a rogue hotspot it saw before.
