Phishing Simulation

Step through each stage of a phishing attack to see how it actually works, from the first fake email to the stolen password. Nothing here is real — "SecurePay" is a made-up brand built just for this walkthrough.

Step 1 of 6

1. The Bait

The attacker sets up a fake identity before sending anything.

New Message (attacker's view)

From: "SecurePay Support" <security@securepay-alerts.com>

Subject: Your account has been limited

Red flags at this stage

  • • The display name says "SecurePay Support" — a trusted-sounding brand name.
  • • The real domain, securepay-alerts.com, isn't SecurePay's actual domain.

2. It Lands

The email arrives. In a crowded inbox, it doesn't stand out at all.

PN

Priya Nair

Notes from today's planning meeting

9:14 AM
SP

SecurePay Support

Your account has been limited

9:41 AM
AM

Amazon.com

Your order has shipped

10:02 AM

Red flags at this stage

  • • Nothing, yet — that's the point. It looks exactly like every other email in the list.

3. The Hook

Opening the email reveals manufactured urgency.

Your account has been limited

Dear Customer,

We noticed unusual activity on your account. Your access has been limited until you verify your identity.

Failure to verify within 24 hours will result in permanent suspension.

Red flags at this stage

  • • A generic "Dear Customer" greeting instead of a real name.
  • • An artificial deadline ("24 hours") designed to make you act before you think.

4. The Trap

Clicking the button opens a login page that looks trustworthy — except for one thing.

https://securepay-alerts.com/login

SecurePay

Sign in to your account

Email
Password

Red flags at this stage

  • • The page design looks legitimate — that's expected, it's easy to copy.
  • • The address bar is the one thing that can't be faked: securepay-alerts.com is not SecurePay's real domain.

5. The Catch

The moment credentials are typed in, they belong to the attacker.

SecurePay

you@example.com
••••••••

Redirecting you to SecurePay…

Red flags at this stage

  • • The password is now stored on the attacker's server, not SecurePay's.
  • • A convincing "redirecting" message sends the victim to the real site so nothing seems wrong.

6. The Damage

What happens next — and what should have happened instead.

What happens next

  • • The attacker logs into the real account and locks the victim out.
  • • The same email gets sent to the victim's contacts.
  • • Any reused password gets tried on other accounts too.

What to do instead

  • • Hover a link to preview the real destination before clicking.
  • • Check the sender's actual domain, not just the display name.
  • • Never enter credentials after clicking a link from an email — go to the site directly instead.
  • • Turn on multi-factor authentication.
